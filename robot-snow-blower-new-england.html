What Makes New England Winters Challenging

New England snow is genuinely different from snow in most other parts of the country. Here's what makes it hard on any snow removal equipment:

  • Heavy, wet snow: CT snow is often dense and moisture-heavy — the kind that breaks snow blower shear pins and exhausts gas-powered machines
  • Unpredictable volume: We get everything from 1-inch dustings to 24-inch Nor'easters, sometimes in the same week
  • Freeze-thaw cycles: Snow melts partially, refreezes into ice, then gets buried under the next storm — creating dense packed layers
  • Temperature swings: From 45°F mid-day to -5°F overnight — equipment needs to handle the full range
  • Plow berms: Town plows leave compacted, icy berms at the end of driveways — often the hardest snow to clear
  • Long season: CT averages 35–50 inches per year across 20–25 events from November through March
❄️ Connecticut Snow Stats What a typical CT winter looks like: South Windsor averages about 40 inches of snow per year across roughly 20–22 measurable snow events. Most events are under 6 inches. Major storms (12+ inches) happen 2–4 times per season. Extreme Nor'easters (18+ inches) happen maybe once every 2–3 years.

The Wet Snow Question

This is the question that matters most in New England. CT snow isn't light and fluffy — it's often dense, heavy, and wet. The kind that clogs single-stage electric blowers and breaks shear pins on underpowered gas machines.

The best robot snow blowers use a 2-stage auger system specifically designed for wet, packed, and heavy snow. In testing, the Yarbo handled 12 inches of heavy lake-effect snow without clogging, thanks to its non-clog metal discharge chute and high-strength Q355 steel construction.

The key is the 2-stage design: the first stage auger breaks up and feeds snow in, while the second stage impeller throws it out through the chute. This two-step process is far more effective on wet, dense CT snow than single-stage designs that try to do both at once.

📍 CT Homeowner Tip Run your robot blower incrementally during storms rather than waiting until a storm ends. By clearing 3–4 inches at a time throughout the storm, you avoid dealing with deep, packed accumulation that challenges any blower. The Yarbo does this automatically — it detects snowfall and starts clearing without any input from you.

During the January 2026 Nor'easter that dropped 10–19 inches from Maryland to Maine, documented Yarbo users reported mixed results. Use the robot for the 20+ typical storms per season and manual backup for the 1–2 monster storms. That combination gives you the best of both worlds.

One important tip from experienced users: the Yarbo handles incremental clearing during a storm far better than end-of-storm clearing. If it runs every 2–3 hours during a 24-hour storm, it manages depth much better than trying to clear 18 inches at once when the storm ends.

Connecticut-Specific Factors to Know

Driveway surface matters

Robot snow blowers work best on paved driveways (concrete or asphalt). For gravel driveways, the robot's sensors can get confused by loose material. If you have a gravel driveway, check the specific model's gravel compatibility before purchasing.

GPS setup requirements

The Yarbo requires a 120-degree unobstructed sky view for its RTK GPS antenna. If your property has heavy tree canopy or is surrounded by tall structures, test GPS lock before purchasing. Most suburban CT properties don't have this issue.

Town plow berms

This is the most common complaint from CT Yarbo users. The berm left by the town plow at the end of your driveway is dense, compacted, and often mixed with sand and salt. For standard berms (under 12 inches high), the Yarbo manages fine. For large berms after major storms, you may need to break them up manually first.

Storage

Store your robot snow blower in a garage or basement between storms and at the end of the season. Don't leave it outside overnight in sub-zero temperatures. Charge the battery to 50–70% for long-term storage.
